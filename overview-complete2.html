Speech Animation Framework for Information Structure Research

A browser-based framework for production and perception studies on information structure using simple 2D animated events.

Simple shapes, complex interpretations

In their seminal experiment in 1944, Heider and Simmel revealed that humans have a pronounced tendency to impose narrative meaning even in the presence of simple animations of geometric shapes. Despite the shapes having no discernible features or emotions, participants attributed strong social context, meaningful interactions, and even emotions to them. This experiment, run on traditional 2D displays has since had a significant impact on fields ranging from psychology to narrative storytelling.

What can simple shapes tell us about language?

For linguistic research, we can take this idea one step further. The social, narrative, and discourse context surrounding an event can be systematically changed while the visual materials remain simple and controlled. These contextual changes can affect what speakers choose to mention, what they treat as given or new, what they contrast or correct, and how they formulate their description of the event. Therefore the methodology is particularly well suited (although not limited) for research on information structure.

Crucially, the same contexts can be presented to speakers of different languages. This makes it possible to investigate whether the same contextual manipulation changes speakers' descriptions in different ways across languages, and how those differences are expressed through prosody, word order, syntax, morphology, lexical choice, or combinations of these.

Instead of giving participants sentences to read or repeat, the framework creates controlled situations and lets speakers decide how to describe them.

The framework provides a controlled way to investigate how information structure is encoded across languages and cultures, and how speakers use prosody, word order, syntax, morphology, and lexical choice to express the same underlying discourse distinctions. It is explicitly intended for use in cross-linguistic research. Only minimal adaptations are needed to apply the same experimental paradigm across languages. This provides a shared methodology for investigating information structure while allowing each language to recruit its own linguistic resources, including prosody, word order, syntax, morphology, and focus-sensitive particles. Such methodological comparability is particularly valuable in information-structure research, where differences in experimental design and operationalization can make findings difficult to compare across studies and may contribute to conflicting results even within the same language. The use of this type of animation for cross-linguistic research for information structure was introduced by Beth Ann Hockey (1998), who adapted the paradigm by incorporating properties such as colour and size to investigate the realization and interpretation of focus in English and Hungarian. Find her dissertation on the following link: https://web.mit.edu/~flemming/www/paper/hockey_selections.pdf

The context can be set up in many ways

Including but not limited to the use of questions under discussion in order to elicit various categories of information structure. Questions under discussion (QUDs) can explicitly guide speakers towards particular aspects of an event, referents, properties, or focus types. Alternatively, the animations themselves can establish the relevant discourse conditions through preceding events, contrasts, expectations, and interactions between objects. These approaches can also be combined within the same experimental design.

Why shapes?

Simple 2D shapes provide a controlled visual stimuli in which shape, colour, size, movement, and interaction can be manipulated independently. The same basic materials can be adapted to many information-structural questions.

Broad focus

A broad question such as “What happened?” can be used to elicit broad focus, where the whole event is informative.

What happened?

A triangle showed up.

New-information focus

A new referent can be introduced into an already established visual context. This allows discourse-new material to be elicited even later in the experiment.

What showed up?

A square showed up.

Narrow focus

A specific constituent can be targeted by the context question. Here, the question elicits narrow focus on the subject.

Which shape pushed the other one off the screen?

The square pushed the triangle off the screen.

Contrastive focus

Explicit alternatives can make the contrast set clear. Here, the question contrasts the circle and the square directly.

Which shape jumped: the circle or the square?

The square jumped.

Corrective focus

A false prior assumption can be corrected explicitly. Here, the same event supports corrective focus.

The circle jumped, didn't it?

No. The square jumped.

Focus on object properties: colour

Information structure can target individual object properties. Shape, colour, and size can be manipulated independently or in combination.

What colour is the square?

The square is red.

Verum focus

Here, the context elicits verum focus.

What is it that the circle did?

The circle grew.

Surprise and mirativity

Unusual events can be used to study surprise, unexpectedness, and mirative interpretations.

What happened?!

The circle ate the square.

Use the framework

The framework is designed to be reused and extended. Animations, discourse contexts, elicitation questions, target utterances, and information-structural conditions can all be modified.

Experiments can be run locally for in-person research or deployed online through university servers, JATOS, MindProbe, or other web servers. Online deployment makes the framework suitable for collecting large production and perception datasets from hundreds of participants.

Source code, documentation, and example experiments are available on GitHub.
